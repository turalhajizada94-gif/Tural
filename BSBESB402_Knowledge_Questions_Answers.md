## Question 1

*Review Division 8, Part 1.5 of the Corporations Act 2001.*

### a. Outline the six basic rights of a shareholder who has purchased at least 5% of shares in a company.

**i.** **Attend and speak at general meetings of the company.** The shareholder is entitled to receive notice of general meetings and to attend and be heard on the business put before the meeting.

**ii.** **Vote on key issues put to the members.** This includes voting on ordinary resolutions (decided by a simple majority) and special resolutions (requiring at least 75% of the votes cast), such as appointing a new director, removing an existing director, changing the company's constitution or changing the company name.

**iii.** **Require the directors to call and hold a general meeting, or call one themselves.** This right belongs specifically to members holding at least 5% of the votes that may be cast at a general meeting. Under s 249D the directors must call and arrange the meeting on request (within 21 days of the request, with the meeting held within two months); if they fail to do so, members with at least 5% of the votes may call and hold the meeting themselves under s 249F. Members with at least 5% of the votes may also give notice of a resolution they propose to move (s 249N) and require the company to circulate a members' statement (s 249P).

**iv.** **Sell or transfer their shares.** Shares are the shareholder's property and may be sold or transferred, although in a proprietary company this right is usually restricted by the constitution (for example, by pre-emptive rights requiring the shares to be offered to existing members first, or by director approval of transfers).

**v.** **Participate in corporate actions.** The shareholder is entitled to take part in actions affecting the share capital of the company, such as new issues of shares (including any pre-emptive right to take up a proportionate share of a new issue), share buy-backs, capital reductions, restructures and mergers.

**vi.** **Receive dividends and other distributions.** Where the directors declare or determine a dividend, the shareholder is entitled to their proportionate share of it, and to share in any surplus assets distributed if the company is wound up.

### b. Briefly explain the limited liability of shareholders who are also not a director.

Because a company is a separate legal entity from the people who own it (s 124), the company's debts and obligations belong to the company itself, not to its members. A shareholder who is not a director is therefore **not personally liable for the company's debts in their capacity as a shareholder**. Their only financial obligation is to pay the company any amount that remains unpaid on their shares if they are called on to do so (s 516). In practical terms, the maximum the shareholder can lose is the money they have paid, or agreed to pay, for their shares — their personal assets such as their home and savings are not at risk if the company fails.

Because they are not a director, they also do not carry directors' duties or the personal exposure that goes with them, such as liability for insolvent trading (s 588G) or liability for unpaid PAYG withholding and superannuation under a director penalty notice. The main circumstances in which a non-director shareholder can still be personally exposed are where they have signed a personal guarantee for a company loan or lease, or where they hold partly paid shares.

## Question 2

*Review the Associations Incorporation legislation applicable to your state/territory — NSW: **Associations Incorporation Act 2009 (NSW)**.*

### a. Identify the minimum number of investors that an incorporated association requires.

An incorporated association must have a **minimum of five (5) members** at all times, and must also have a management committee of **at least three (3) members** who are over 18 years of age (with at least three residing in Australia). There is no maximum number of members. If the association's membership falls below five, NSW Fair Trading may cancel its incorporation.

### b. Briefly explain how liabilities are distributed in an incorporated association.

On incorporation the association becomes a **separate legal entity in its own right**, distinct from the people who make it up. This means all liabilities sit with the association itself, not with its individual members: the association is responsible for its own debts, its contracts and any claims made against it (for example, a claim for negligence), and it can sue and be sued in its own name.

A **member's liability is limited to what they owe the association for their membership** — usually any outstanding fees or subscriptions. Members cannot be pursued personally for the association's debts.

**Committee members** are similarly protected from personal liability for acts carried out in good faith and with due care and diligence in the proper performance of their role. That protection is lost where a committee member acts dishonestly or recklessly, breaches their statutory duties, allows the association to incur debts while insolvent, or gives a personal guarantee.

### c. Briefly explain the taxation responsibilities of an incorporated association.

An incorporated association is treated as a taxpayer in its own right and must meet the following obligations:

- **Register for an ABN** and use it in its dealings with the ATO, suppliers and funding bodies.
- **Income tax** — a genuinely not-for-profit association may be able to self-assess as income tax exempt (or, if it is a charity, must register with the ACNC and be endorsed by the ATO as income tax exempt). Non-exempt associations, and associations that generate profit-based income, must lodge an annual income tax return and pay income tax on their assessable income.
- **GST** — must register for GST if its GST turnover reaches the not-for-profit threshold of **$150,000** (or $75,000 if it is not a not-for-profit body), then charge GST on taxable supplies, lodge activity statements and claim GST credits on creditable purchases.
- **PAYG withholding and Single Touch Payroll** — if it employs staff or pays contractors without an ABN, it must withhold tax from those payments, report through STP and pay the amounts withheld to the ATO. Superannuation guarantee obligations also apply to employees.
- **Fringe benefits tax (FBT)** — if it provides fringe benefits to employees, it must calculate, report and pay FBT, although some not-for-profit associations qualify for FBT rebates or exemptions.
- **Record keeping** — must keep all financial and tax records in plain English (or a form the ATO can readily access) for at least **five years**, and lodge annual financial reporting with NSW Fair Trading.

### e. Briefly explain what a fringe benefit is using your own words.

A fringe benefit is a **non-cash benefit that an employer gives to an employee (or to the employee's family or another associate) because of their employment**, instead of, or in addition to, paying them salary or wages. It is something of personal value that the worker receives through their job rather than money in their pay packet — for example, the private use of a company car, a car parking space, paying an employee's gym membership, private health insurance or school fees, providing entertainment such as meals and tickets, a low-interest or interest-free loan, a discounted product, or reimbursing an employee's private expenses. Because it is a form of remuneration, the employer (not the employee) pays fringe benefits tax on the grossed-up value of the benefit.

### f. Outline the seven steps required to calculate for fringe benefits tax (FBT).

**i.** **Step 1** — Work out the **taxable value of each fringe benefit** you provided to each employee during the FBT year (1 April to 31 March), using the valuation rules that apply to that type of benefit and taking into account any reductions, exemptions and employee contributions.

**ii.** **Step 2** — Add up the taxable values from Step 1 for **all the benefits for which you can claim a GST credit** (the "type 1" benefits).

**iii.** **Step 3** — Multiply the Step 2 total by the **higher (type 1) gross-up rate of 2.0802** to get the grossed-up taxable value of those benefits.

**iv.** **Step 4** — Add up the taxable values from Step 1 for **all the benefits for which you cannot claim a GST credit** (the "type 2" benefits — GST-free or input-taxed benefits).

**v.** **Step 5** — Multiply the Step 4 total by the **lower (type 2) gross-up rate of 1.8868** to get the grossed-up taxable value of those benefits.

**vi.** **Step 6** — **Add the grossed-up amounts from Step 3 and Step 5** together. This total is your fringe benefits taxable amount.

**vii.** **Step 7** — Multiply the Step 6 fringe benefits taxable amount by the **FBT rate of 47%**. The result is the total FBT you must pay when you lodge your FBT return.

### g. Outline the two evidences that records relating to FBT return must be able to show.

**i.** The **taxable value of each fringe benefit provided to each employee** during the FBT year.

**ii.** **How that taxable value was calculated** — the valuation method used and the working behind it, including any reduction, exemption or concession claimed (for example, the "otherwise deductible" rule) and any employee contributions taken into account.

### h. Outline five types of records that can be used to support an FBT return.

**i.** **Calculations and worksheets** showing how the taxable value and grossed-up amounts for each benefit were worked out.

**ii.** **Employee declarations and elections** — for example, declarations supporting the "otherwise deductible" rule, living-away-from-home declarations, and elections about the valuation method used.

**iii.** **Invoices, receipts, bills of sale and purchase receipts** — including purchase and servicing/maintenance receipts for vehicles.

**iv.** **Lease documents and other agreements** for cars, equipment or property provided as benefits.

**v.** **Travel diaries, logbooks, odometer records and fleet management records**, and any valuations obtained where a valuation is required.

*(All FBT records must be kept for five years from the date they are prepared, obtained, or the transaction is completed.)*

## Question 6

### a. Briefly explain how to conduct each step of the risk management process.

| Risk Management Process | Explanation of How to Conduct Each Step of the Risk Management Process |
|---|---|
| **i. Identify risks** | Work systematically through each business activity and work process — sales, marketing, finance, accounting, customer service and human resources — and ask what could go wrong, where and when it could happen, who and what would be affected, and why it might occur. Separate internal risks (human-factor, technological and physical) from external risks (economic, social, political, legal, regulatory, environmental, supply chain and stakeholder factors). Gather the information by inspecting the workplace and work processes, reviewing incident reports, audit findings and historical data, consulting workers, managers and external experts, considering similar events in the industry, and thinking through the worst-case scenario. Record every risk found in a risk register so nothing is lost. |
| **ii. Analyse risks** | For each risk in the register, work out **how likely** it is to occur and **how severe** the consequences would be if it did. Use historical data, expert opinion and predictive or simulation techniques to rate likelihood on a scale from Rare (<5%) through Unlikely, Possible and Likely to Almost Certain (>90%), and rate the impact from Insignificant through Minor, Moderate and Major to Severe using clear descriptors (for example, impact measured as a percentage of budget). Also identify the existing controls that are already in place and how effective they are, because the analysis should reflect the residual risk that remains after those controls. |
| **iii. Evaluate risks** | Plot the likelihood rating against the impact rating on a risk matrix or risk heat map to arrive at a risk level or risk rating (for example, Risk Level = Impact × Likelihood, giving a score between 1 and 25, expressed as Minimum, Low, Moderate, High or Critical). Compare each rating with the organisation's predetermined risk acceptance threshold to decide which risks are acceptable and which must be treated, then rank the risks in priority order so that the most severe and most likely risks are dealt with first and resources are not wasted on negligible risks. |
| **iv. Treat risks** | For each risk requiring treatment, evaluate the available treatment options — avoid, reduce, transfer or accept — and decide which is the most suitable and affordable given the business's current circumstances. Document the decision in a risk treatment plan that records the risk, the treatment option and action chosen, the personnel responsible, the target date for implementation and the expected outcome. Then implement the treatment, applying the hierarchy of controls where the risk is a WHS hazard, and making any necessary adjustments to business structure, finances, resourcing and stakeholder communication to support it. |
| **v. Monitoring and reviewing risks** | Put a monitoring scheme in place that keeps the treated risk under observation, and check at scheduled intervals whether the treatment has actually controlled or eliminated the risk, whether the risk has changed or recurred, and whether new risks have emerged. Use audits, inspections, incident reports and consultation with staff as monitoring tools. If a control has not reduced the risk to an acceptable level, select and implement a different treatment. Update the risk register and risk treatment plan with the results, and review the whole process whenever legislation, business activities, technology or the operating environment change. |

### b. Briefly explain how each option for treating risk is applied in the workplace.

| Risk Treatment Options | Explanation of How Each Option is Applied in the Workplace |
|---|---|
| **i. Avoid risk** | The business decides not to undertake, or to stop undertaking, the activity that creates the risk, because the risk is unnecessary or outweighs the benefit. In practice this means having enough information and business know-how to say "no" to an opportunity — for example, declining to launch a new product line, refusing to enter an unproven interstate market, choosing not to store hazardous chemicals on site, or withdrawing a piece of equipment or a service that cannot be operated safely. It eliminates the risk entirely, but it also gives up the potential benefit, so the decision must be based on the assessed probability and severity of the risk. |
| **ii. Reduce risks** | The business keeps the activity but puts control measures in place to lower the likelihood of the risk occurring, the severity of its consequences, or both. In a WHS context this is done by working through the hierarchy of controls — eliminate the hazard if possible, then substitute, isolate or use engineering controls, then administrative controls such as safe work procedures, signage, rostering and staff training, and personal protective equipment as the last line of defence. Other examples include preventative maintenance schedules, backing up data and running antivirus software, credit checks on new customers, quality assurance checks, and business continuity planning. |
| **iii. Transfer risk** | The business shifts some or all of the financial consequence of the risk to a third party, most commonly through **insurance** or **outsourcing/contracting**. The business identifies the exposures it cannot afford to carry and takes out appropriate cover — public liability, professional indemnity, income protection, workers compensation, product liability, property and business interruption insurance — or contracts the risky work out to a specialist provider and uses contract terms such as indemnity and warranty clauses to allocate liability. The risk still exists; only the cost of it is shared with the insurer or contractor, so premiums, excesses and contract terms must be checked with a solicitor, insurance broker or accountant. |
| **iv. Accept risk** | The business makes a deliberate, documented decision to retain the risk and deal with the consequences as they arise, because the risk is small and infrequent and the cost of treating it would exceed the benefit. Acceptance is applied by recording the decision in the risk register, confirming that the business has the financial capacity to absorb the consequence (for example, by setting aside a contingency fund or accepting a higher insurance excess), assigning someone to keep monitoring the risk, and setting a review date so that the decision is revisited if the likelihood or impact changes. |

### c. Outline the six requirements that you must include in a risk treatment plan.

**i.** The **identified potential risk** within the business — a clear description of the risk and where in the business it arises.

**ii.** The **risk treatment option** applicable to that risk — avoidance, reduction, transfer or acceptance.

**iii.** The **risk treatment action** decided on — the specific, best-suited action to be implemented to control the risk.

**iv.** The **personnel responsible** for implementing the treatment and for dealing with the risk.

**v.** The **target date of implementation** — when the risk treatment is to be put in place.

**vi.** The **expected outcomes** from implementing the treatment — the result the business expects to achieve, which is used to measure the treatment's effectiveness during monitoring and review.

## Question 7

### Briefly describe each taxation principle outlined in the table below.

| Taxation Principle | Description |
|---|---|
| **i. Canon of Equity** | Tax should be paid according to the taxpayer's **ability to pay**. An organisation settles its taxes in proportion to the income or revenue it generates, so that those who earn more contribute more and the burden is shared fairly across businesses and individuals. In practice this is reflected in progressive tax scales and in taxing business profit rather than turnover. |
| **ii. Canon of Certainty** | The factors required to comply with taxation must be **clear, precise and known in advance** to the people responsible for paying. The taxpayer should be certain of how much tax is payable, how it is calculated, when it must be paid and in what manner, so the business can budget for its liability and is not exposed to arbitrary demands. |
| **iii. Canon of Convenience** | The **manner and timing of paying tax must be convenient** for the taxpayer. Tax should be collected at a time and in a form that suits the way the business earns its income — for example, PAYG instalments and withholding paid as income is earned, quarterly BAS cycles aligned to trading activity, and electronic lodgment and payment options through the ATO's online services. |
| **iv. Canon of Economy** | The cost of collecting and complying with tax should be **as small as possible** relative to the revenue raised. Tax requirements should be clear and concise and not open to interpretation, so that neither the government nor the business wastes money on administration, disputes and unnecessary compliance work — the greater part of what is collected should reach the public revenue rather than being consumed by the cost of collection. |

## Question 9

*Review the Competition and Consumer Act 2010.*

### a. Briefly explain the function of the Competition and Consumer Act 2010.

The *Competition and Consumer Act 2010* (Cth) is the principal national law regulating how businesses deal with their competitors, suppliers and customers. Its function is to **protect consumers and promote fair trading and competition among businesses providing similar products and services**. It does this by regulating the relationships between suppliers, wholesalers, retailers and consumers — prohibiting anti-competitive conduct such as price fixing, cartels and misuse of market power, and prohibiting unfair practices such as misleading or deceptive conduct, false representations and unconscionable conduct.

Schedule 2 of the Act contains the **Australian Consumer Law**, which sets out consumer guarantees, product safety rules, unfair contract term protections and remedies (repair, replacement or refund) that apply nationally. The Act is administered by the Australian Competition and Consumer Commission (ACCC), together with state and territory fair trading agencies such as NSW Fair Trading, which can investigate breaches and seek penalties, injunctions and compensation.

### b. Outline the five areas of consumer rights that the Competition and Consumer Act protects.

**i.** **Unfair contract terms**, covering standard form consumer contracts.

**ii.** **Consumer rights when buying goods and services** (the consumer guarantees and the remedies that apply when they are not met).

**iii.** **Product safety** — including safety standards, bans and mandatory recall and reporting obligations.

**iv.** **Unsolicited consumer agreements**, covering door-to-door sales and telephone sales.

**v.** **Lay-by agreements.**

### c. Outline three guarantees of a consumer that apply to goods that are sold through trade or commerce.

**i.** Suppliers and manufacturers guarantee that the **goods are of acceptable quality** when sold to a consumer — that is, safe, durable, free from defects, acceptable in appearance and finish, and fit for all the purposes for which goods of that kind are commonly supplied.

**ii.** Suppliers and manufacturers guarantee that their **description of the goods is accurate** (for example, in a catalogue, on packaging or in a television commercial), and that the goods will **satisfy any extra promises made about them** (express warranties).

**iii.** A supplier guarantees that they have the **right to sell the goods (clear title)** unless the consumer was alerted before the sale that they had limited title, that no one will try to repossess or take back the goods or prevent the consumer using them except in certain circumstances, and that the goods are **free of any hidden securities or charges** and will remain so, except in certain circumstances.

*(Other guarantees include that the goods will match any sample or demonstration model and any description provided, and that manufacturers or importers will take reasonable steps to provide spare parts and repair facilities for a reasonable time after purchase.)*
